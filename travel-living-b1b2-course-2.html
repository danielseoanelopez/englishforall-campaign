C · Grammar — Relative clauses — one sentence instead of two

They let you say exactly which person, thing or place: I want an apartment. It has a balcony. → I want an apartment that has a balcony.

Puente al español · de «que» a cinco palabras
  • En español, que hace casi todo el trabajo. En inglés se reparte: personas → who, cosas → which, y that sirve para las dos. Si dudas, usa that.
  • En español el «que» nunca desaparece; en inglés, muchas veces sí: el departamento que vimos = the apartment (that) we saw. Al escuchar, a menudo no vas a oír ningún that.
  • «en la que», «con quien»: en inglés la preposición va al final. La ciudad en la que vivo = the city I live in = the city where I live — pero nunca las dos juntas. La persona con quien vivo = the person I live with.
  • «lo que» no es that, es what: Lo que necesito es un balcón = What I need is a balcony. That I need is a balcony.
  • cuyo, cuya, cuyos, cuyas → siempre whose. No cambia nunca.
You can drop it… sometimesIf a new subject follows, that / which / who can disappear:
The apartment (that) we saw was perfect.
If it is the subject, it stays:
The apartment that has a balcony…
Watch outThe apartment that I saw it was nice. The apartment that I saw was nice. no repitas "it"
the landlord which… the landlord who…

B2 stretch · extra information, with commas
Non-defining clauses add information the listener doesn't need to identify the thing — they go between commas and never use that: "The apartment, which is near the park, costs $1,400 a month." · "My landlord, who lives upstairs, fixes everything fast." My landlord, that lives upstairs…

Model Dialogue · calling about an apartment

Ivy: Hi, I'm calling about the one-bedroom on Maple Street. Is it still available?

Landlord: It is. It's a bright apartment that gets sun all afternoon.

Ivy: Great. How much is the rent, and are utilities included?

Landlord: Thirteen hundred a month. Water is included, but electricity isn't.

Ivy: OK. And how much is the security deposit?

Landlord: One month's rent. The lease is for twelve months.

Ivy: I work from home, so I need a place where it's quiet during the day. What's the building like?

Landlord: Very quiet. Most of the tenants are people who work downtown all day.

Ivy: Perfect. Is there laundry in the building?

Landlord: Yes, in the basement. And there's a park across the street where people walk their dogs.

Ivy: So… are pets allowed?

Landlord: Small ones, yes. Would you like to come see it on Saturday?

Ivy: Saturday at ten works. Thank you!

Q1. How much is the rent, and which utility is included?

Q2. Why does Ivy need a quiet building?

Q3. Find three relative clauses in the dialogue. Which word does each one use?

Answer key · comprehension
  1. $1,300 a month; water is included (electricity is not).
  2. She works from home.
  3. "…an apartment that gets sun…" · "…a place where it's quiet…" · "…people who work downtown…" · "…a park where people walk their dogs."

The student must ask at least five questions and describe what they need with three relative clauses, then decide: take it or keep looking?

If the student needs support, start from this frame:

Hi, I'm calling about the apartment on street. I'm looking for a place that has / is…, in a neighborhood where I can…. How much …? Are … included? Can I come see it when?
Teacher note — landlord's card: $1,450/month · utilities not included · deposit = two months · twelve-month lease · no pets · laundromat two blocks away · available October 1. Don't volunteer the deposit or the pet rule — the student only finds the catch if they ask. Debrief: "Would you take it? Why not?"
Homework
Write an online post (6–8 sentences): either "Apartment wanted" or "Roommate wanted". Describe the place or the person you're looking for and the neighborhood you want. Use at least four relative clauses — and try all of who, that, where and whose.

US The date trap
On American forms the month comes first: 07/04/2026 = July 4, not April 7. Write MM/DD/YYYY unless the form says otherwise — a wrong date of birth can get a form rejected. And in the US you fill out a form.

Model Dialogue · opening a bank account

Clerk: Good morning. How can I help you?

Nao: Hi. I just moved here, and I'd like to open a checking account. Could you tell me what documents I need?

Clerk: Sure. We need a photo ID and proof of address.

Nao: I have my passport. Do you know if a lease works as proof of address?

Clerk: Yes, a signed lease is fine.

Nao: Great. Can you tell me if there's a monthly fee?

Clerk: It's ten dollars a month — but there's no fee if you deposit more than five hundred a month.

Nao: OK. And do you know when my debit card will arrive?

Clerk: In seven to ten days, by mail. Could you fill out this form, please? Sign at the bottom.

Nao: Sure. Sorry — could you show me where the date goes?

Clerk: Right here. Month first, then the day, then the year.

Nao: Month first. Good to know — thanks!

Q1. Which two documents does Nao need, and what does she use for each?

Q2. How can she avoid the monthly fee?

Q3. What almost goes wrong on the form?

Answer key · comprehension
  1. A photo ID (her passport) and proof of address (her signed lease).
  2. By depositing more than $500 a month.
  3. The date — in the US the month goes first.

American thermometers use °F. 98.6°F = 37°C (normal) · 100.4°F = 38°C (fever starts) · 102°F ≈ 39°C · 104°F = 40°C (see a doctor). Exact: °C = (°F − 32) × 5 ÷ 9.

Model Dialogue · at the pharmacy

Pharmacist: Hi there. What can I do for you?

Ada: Hi. I've had a sore throat and a fever since yesterday. Can you recommend something?

Pharmacist: How high is the fever?

Ada: About a hundred and one.

Pharmacist: OK. Take these for the fever — two tablets every six hours. Look, the label says you must not take more than eight in a day.

Ada: Do I have to take them with food?

Pharmacist: You don't have to, but it's easier on your stomach. And you should drink lots of water.

Ada: Should I see a doctor?

Pharmacist: If the fever is still high in two days, you'd better go to urgent care. You don't need an appointment there.

Ada: Great. Oh — one more thing. I think I left my passport in a taxi. What should I do?

Pharmacist: You'd better call the taxi company first. If it's gone, you have to file a police report, and then contact your consulate.

Ada: Thank you — you've been really helpful.

Q1. What are Ada's symptoms, and since when?

Q2. What are the two rules for the tablets? Which one is obligatory and which one isn't?

Q3. What are the steps for the lost passport, in order?

Answer key · comprehension
  1. A sore throat and a fever (about 101°F ≈ 38.3°C), since yesterday.
  2. Max. eight tablets a day — obligatory (must not). Taking them with food — not necessary (don't have to), just recommended.
  3. Call the taxi company → if it's gone, file a police report → contact the consulate.

B2 stretch · would for past routines
For repeated past actions (not states), would sounds natural in stories: "Every Sunday we would eat at my grandmother's." But I would live in Lima → I used to live in Lima (a state). Also: "I've gotten used to it" (US) · "I'll never get used to the winters here."
Controlled Practice 15 min
Activity 1 · used to, be used to or get used to? — use the verb in brackets
  1. I ___ (live) with my parents, but now I have my own apartment.
  2. After two years here, I ___ (drive) on these big highways.
  3. It's hard, but I'm slowly ___ (speak) English all day.
  4. ___ you ___ (have) a big family lunch every Sunday back home?
  5. She can't ___ (eat) dinner at six — it feels so early!
  6. I ___ (not / like) coffee, but now I drink three cups a day.
  7. Don't worry. You'll ___ (be) far from home — it gets easier.
  8. I ___ (be) shy, but moving here changed me.
Answer key · Activity 1
  1. used to live
  2. am used to driving (also: have gotten used to driving)
  3. getting used to speaking
  4. Did you use to have
  5. get used to eating
  6. didn't use to like
  7. get used to being
  8. used to be

Model Dialogue · one year later

Leo: So how long have you been here now? A year?

Max: A year next week. It went so fast.

Leo: Are you used to the winters yet?

Max: Not really! Back home I used to go to the beach in December. Here I wear three jackets.

Leo: Ha! What about the food?

Max: I'm getting used to eating dinner at six. I used to eat at ten!

Leo: And work? You used to hate your commute.

Max: I did. Now I bike to work, and I'm totally used to it. I actually love it.

Leo: Do you still get homesick?

Max: Sometimes. But I've made some friends at the climbing gym, and that helped a lot.

Leo: That's great. A few of us are going to a concert on Friday. Want to come?

Max: I'd love to! Send me the details.

Q1. What did Max use to do in December?

Q2. What is he still getting used to, and what is he already used to?

Q3. What helped him with feeling homesick?

Answer key · comprehension
  1. He used to go to the beach.
  2. Still getting used to: eating dinner at six (and the winters). Already used to: biking to work.
  3. Making friends at the climbing gym.
Free Practice · Produce 15 min

Part 1 — "then and now" interview. The tutor interviews the student about a real change in their life: a move, a new job, a new school, becoming a parent. Question cards:

  • What did you use to do that you don't do now?
  • What was hard at first that you're used to now?
  • What are you still getting used to?
  • What do you think you'll never get used to?

Part 2 — the new neighbor. The tutor plays a neighbor the student meets in the elevator. Small talk → find something in common → make a real plan with a day and time. (Recycle Part 1: future forms — "I'm meeting some friends on Saturday…")

I moved to place in year. Back home, I used to verb. At first it was hard to verb, but now I'm used to verb + -ing. I'm still getting used to noun / -ing. The best thing here is ….
Teacher note: Under pressure, almost everyone says "I'm used to wake up…". When you hear it, just repeat the sentence back with the -ing and keep going — don't break the conversation. This is the course finale: in Part 2 of the role-play, expect and praise language from all eight lessons.
Homework · course finale
Write a letter to yourself on your first day in a new place (8–10 sentences) — real or imagined. Tell your past self what you used to worry about, what you're used to now, what you're still getting used to, and give one piece of advice with should or had better. Bonus: one relative clause (a friend who…, a place where…).
