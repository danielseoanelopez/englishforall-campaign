Grammar focusCleft sentences

A cleft ("split") sentence divides one idea into two clauses so you can put the spotlight on one part of it. It's how fluent speakers emphasise a point without raising their voice.

StructureNeutral → Emphatic
It-cleft: It + be + X + that/who…"The feedback frustrates people." → It's the feedback that frustrates people, not the workload.
What-cleft: What… + be + X"Trust matters here." → What really matters here is trust.
The reason / the thing + be…"I disagree because it's rushed." → The reason I disagree is that it's rushed.
All + clause + be…"I just want to wait." → All I'm saying is we should wait.

⚠️ Two common slips: What I want is to you listen. → What I want is for you to listen. · It's the price what matters. → It's the price that matters.

C2 stretch · same structure, harder forms
Reversed wh-cleft: "Trust is what matters here." · Negative it-cleft: "It wasn't until the meeting that I understood." · Cleft with a clause: "What surprised me was that nobody objected."
✏️ Controlled Practice 15 min
Activity 1 · Rewrite with a cleft sentence for emphasis
  1. I really admire her honesty. → What ___
  2. We need more time, not more money. → What ___
  3. His attitude annoys me, not his work. → It ___
  4. I only realized the mistake much later. → It ___
  5. I just want a clear answer. → All ___
Answer key · Activity 1 (sample answers)
  1. What I really admire (about her) is her honesty.
  2. What we need is more time, not more money.
  3. It's his attitude that annoys me, not his work.
  4. It was only much later that I realized the mistake.
  5. All I want is a clear answer.
Activity 2 · Put them straight — someone has misunderstood you. Correct them with a cleft sentence (X, not Y).

This is what cleft sentences are for: contradicting a wrong assumption without sounding aggressive.

  1. They say: "So the team missed the deadline because they're disorganised."
    The truth: the brief changed three times. → ___
  2. They say: "You didn't like the design, then."
    The truth: you liked the design — it was the colours. → ___
  3. They say: "So you're asking for more money."
    The truth: you need more time. → ___
  4. They say: "You want to cancel the whole campaign?"
    The truth: you only want to delay the launch. → ___
  5. They say: "You're blaming me for the mix-up."
    The truth: you blame the system, not any person. → ___
Answer key · Activity 2 (sample answers — accept any correct cleft that contrasts X with Y)
  1. "It's the brief that caused the delay, not the team — it changed three times." / "What caused the delay was the brief changing three times."
  2. "No — it's the colours I wasn't sure about, not the design." / "What I didn't like was the colours."
  3. "Actually, what we need is more time, not more money." / "It's time that we're short of."
  4. "All I'm suggesting is that we delay the launch." / "What I'm proposing is a delay, not a cancellation."
  5. "Not at all — it's the system that failed here, not you." / "What went wrong was the process, not anything you did."
Teacher note: the "X, not Y" frame is the fastest route to a natural cleft — the contrast supplies the reason to emphasise. If a student answers without the contrast ("We need more time"), point out that the sentence is correct but doesn't correct the misunderstanding.
Activity 3 · Say it diplomatically — match each blunt line (1–4) to its diplomatic version (a–d). Write the letter.
  1. "Your plan won't work."  →  ___
  2. "That's a stupid idea."  →  ___
  3. "You didn't do the research properly."  →  ___
  4. "No. We're doing it my way."  →  ___
Diplomatic versions — one fits each line
  • a. "With respect, the research may need another look. What's missing is the data on the second group."
  • b. "Admittedly there's a lot to like here, but I'm not entirely convinced it'll work. What worries me is the timeline."
  • c. "I see it rather differently, and arguably both could work. All I'm suggesting is that we try my approach first."
  • d. "I take your point, though I'd question whether that's the best route. It's the cost that concerns me most."

Then: in each diplomatic version, underline the cleft sentence and say what it emphasises.

Now your turn — write a fifth pair of your own

Think of something blunt you've heard (or said) at work. Write it down, then rewrite it diplomatically using (a) a hedge or concession from the vocabulary and (b) a cleft sentence.

Blunt: "…"
Diplomatic: "hedge / concession, but …. What … is / It's … that / All I'm …."
Answer key · Activity 3

1-b · 2-d · 3-a · 4-c

The clefts: b) What worries me is the timeline — emphasises the specific concern, so the criticism isn't total. · d) It's the cost that concerns me most — narrows the objection to one factor. · a) What's missing is the data on the second group — names the gap instead of blaming the person. · c) All I'm suggesting is that we try my approach first — makes the demand sound minimal.

Model answer · "Now your turn"
Blunt: "You're always late with the reports."
Diplomatic: "Admittedly we've all been stretched this month, and I'm not blaming anyone. It's the reporting deadline that keeps slipping, and what I'd like to agree on is a date we can both realistically stick to."
Both ingredients are there: a concession first (admittedly… I'm not blaming anyone), then a cleft that shifts the complaint from the person to the deadline — which is what makes it land as a problem to solve rather than an accusation.

Teacher note: the matching itself is quick — the value is in the two follow-ups. Every cleft here does the same diplomatic job: it narrows a broad attack into one specific, fixable point. Draw that out before they write their own pair, and if their rewrite still sounds blunt, ask "what exactly is the problem?" — the answer is usually the cleft.
⭐ Model · a diplomatic disagreement

Dana: I'll be blunt — the training budget is the first thing I'm cutting. Forty thousand a year, and I can't point to one concrete result.

Rob: I take your point on the numbers. Admittedly, we've never measured it properly. But I'd push back on the conclusion.

Dana: Go on.

Rob: What we've been measuring is attendance, not behaviour. People do the course, go back to their desks, and nothing changes. It's the follow-up that's missing, not the training.

Dana: That may well be true, but I can't take "trust me, it works" to the board in March.

Rob: No — and I wouldn't ask you to. All I'm asking for is one more quarter, with proper metrics this time. If it still shows nothing, I'll back down.

Dana: I don't think we're going to see eye to eye on the value of it. But one quarter, and I want the metrics agreed in writing.

Rob: That's fair.

Teacher note: notice that Dana doesn't fold — she stays blunt, sets a condition, and they end up negotiating rather than agreeing. That's what makes the diplomatic language do real work. Ask the student: (1) where does Rob concede before disagreeing? ("I take your point on the numbers… Admittedly…" and "No — and I wouldn't ask you to.") (2) Find the three clefts and say what each one contrasts: attendance ↔ behaviour, follow-up ↔ training, one quarter ↔ everything else he could have demanded. Clefts sound natural here precisely because each corrects a misunderstanding.
🗣️ Free Practice · Produce 15 min

Debate a motion — e.g. "Social media does more harm than good." Argue for or against; the tutor pushes back. Concede at least one point, hedge at least once, and emphasise your key claim with at least two cleft sentences.

I'd argue that claim. Admittedly, concession, but …. What really matters is …. It's X that …, not Y.
Model answer · a debate turn (motion: "Social media does more harm than good")

"Admittedly, the evidence on teenage mental health is troubling, and I wouldn't dismiss it for a second. That said, I'm not entirely convinced the platforms themselves are the root cause. What the research actually points to is unlimited, unsupervised use — not social media as such. It's the design of the feed that does the damage, not the fact of being connected. For people who are isolated, it's arguably a lifeline. So I'd argue we should regulate the mechanics rather than write the whole thing off."

The four ingredients: concede (Admittedly… I wouldn't dismiss it) → hedge (I'm not entirely convinced · arguably) → two clefts that narrow the claim (What the research points to is… · It's the design of the feed that…) → a position (regulate the mechanics rather than write it off). Note that both clefts contrast X, not Y — that's what stops a concession from sounding like surrender.

Teacher note: reward register over volume — a measured "I'm not sure that holds up" beats a forceful "no". Read the model aloud after their first attempt, not before, then have them redo the same turn. If the student is strong, have them switch sides mid-debate (play devil's advocate).
📌 Homework
Write an opinion paragraph (120–150 words) on a topic you feel strongly about. Use at least three cleft sentences and two hedging expressions, concede one opposing point, and include today's word, idiom and phrasal verb.

1 · Functional language — register: phrasal vs. formal (vocabulary, not grammar)

Phrasal verbs are usually the natural, spoken choice; single-word (often Latin-origin) verbs are the formal, written choice. C1 means being able to switch.

Grammar focusPhrasal verbs (multi-word verbs)

The meaning is vocabulary, but the word order is grammar — and it's where advanced students slip. There are four patterns:

TypeRuleExample
1. Intransitive
no object		nothing can come between the partsThe car broke down. · She turned up late.
2. Transitive separableobject can go after or in the middleput off the meeting / put the meeting off
3. Transitive inseparableobject always after the whole verblook after the kids look the kids after
4. Three-word
always inseparable		verb + adverb + prepositionput up with the noise · look forward to seeing you

⚠️ The pronoun rule — the single most important one: with a separable verb, a pronoun object must go in the middle.
Put it off. Put off it. · Turn them down. Turn down them.

C2 stretch · same structure, harder uses
Nouns from phrasal verbs: a breakdown, a setback, the turnout, a handout, an outbreak. · One verb, several meanings: take off = a plane leaves / remove clothing / become successful. · Formal-sounding phrasal verbs do exist: carry out research, point out a problem, bring about change — perfectly acceptable in writing.

⭐ Model · the same message, two registers

Spoken (natural): "So the deal fell through. We'd been putting it off for weeks, and in the end the client turned us down. I'm looking into what went wrong, and we'll go over it in Monday's meeting — I just can't put up with another surprise like that."

Written (formal): "The agreement was not finalised. Following repeated postponements, the client declined our proposal. I am currently investigating the cause and will review the matter at Monday's meeting; a further oversight of this kind would be unacceptable."

Teacher note: same content, opposite registers. Ask which sounds like a colleague and which like a report — then have the student convert a third message both ways. This "two-column" drill is the fastest way to make register conscious.
🗣️ Free Practice · Produce 15 min

Two rounds. (1) Tell it naturally: describe a project, plan or trip that didn't go well, using at least six phrasal verbs (and getting the pronouns right). (2) Say it formally: retell the same story as if reporting it to a manager, swapping the phrasal verbs for formal equivalents.

We'd been -ing, but it fell through. I looked into it and came up with …. In the end we had to put … off and just get by.
Model answer · the same story, two registers

Natural (to a colleague): "So we'd been planning this trip for months, and about a week before, the whole thing fell through. The airline put the flight back twice and then cancelled it altogether. I looked into getting a refund, but they kept putting me off. In the end we had to call the whole thing off and just get by with a weekend at my sister's. Honestly, I'm still trying to get over it."

Formal (to a manager): "Our trip was cancelled a week before departure. The airline postponed the flight twice before cancelling it entirely. I attempted to obtain a refund, but the airline repeatedly delayed its response. We ultimately abandoned the plan and spent the weekend locally."

Seven phrasal verbs in the first version, none in the second — and note put me off and call it off, where the pronoun/object sits in the middle. The formal version is shorter and colder; that's the trade-off worth discussing.

Teacher note: listen specifically for the pronoun rule (put it off, not put off it) and for wrongly separated inseparable verbs. Those two errors are what mark a student as "advanced but not natural".

Grammar focusNarrative tenses

A good anecdote uses four past forms, each with a distinct job. Getting the layers of time right is what makes a story easy to follow.

TenseIts job in the story · example
Past simplethe main events, in order · We landed, took a taxi and checked in.
Past continuousthe scene / an action in progress · The sun was setting and people were heading home.
Past perfectan earlier completed event (a flashback) · By the time we arrived, the shop had closed.
Past perfect continuousa longer earlier action, often explaining a result · We 'd been driving for hours when the car broke down.

⚠️ The classic C1 slip is flattening everything into past simple: When we arrived, the film already started. → …the film had already started.

C2 stretch · same tense system, finer control
used to / would for past habits: "We used to spend summers there." · "Every evening we would sit by the fire." (would only for repeated actions, never states: I would have a car.) · Past perfect for unfulfilled intentions: "I had hoped to finish, but…" · Present tenses for dramatic effect in an oral story: "So I'm standing there, and this guy walks up to me…"

Model answer · a two-minute anecdote

"I'd been working at the same café for about two years, and I was getting pretty bored. It was a slow Tuesday — it was raining, and I was wiping down tables — when, out of the blue, a woman came in and asked if we did catering. I'd never done anything like that before, and I nearly said no. But something made me say yes. To make a long story short, that one conversation turned out to be a turning point: she was organising a wedding, and I ended up catering it. I still have a vivid memory of standing in that kitchen at four in the morning, convinced I'd taken on too much — but it was a stroke of luck that changed my career."

All four tenses are doing distinct work: past perfect continuous for the long background (I'd been working), past continuous for the scene (was raining, was wiping down), past perfect for the flashback (I'd never done), and past simple for the events. Grammar focusMixed conditionals

A mixed conditional joins two different times: usually an unreal past condition with a present result. It's the natural way to talk about how one past decision still shapes your life today.

TypeFormExample
Reference: 3rd conditional
past → past		If + past perfect, would have + p.p.If I had studied medicine, I would have become a doctor.
Mixed A
past condition → present result		If + past perfect, would + baseIf I had studied medicine, I 'd be a doctor now.
Mixed B
present condition → past result		If + past simple, would have + p.p.If I weren't so shy, I would have said something at the meeting.

⚠️ The tense mismatch is the point — don't "correct" it: If I had taken that job, I'd be living in Berlin now. Watch for the classic error If I would have known… → If I had known…

C2 stretch · same structure, advanced forms
Inverted conditionals (drop if): "Had I known, I'd have called." · "Had it not been for her help, I'd still be stuck." · "Were I in your position, I'd wait." · Unreal past with wish/if only: "If only I had listened — I wouldn't be in this mess now." · But for: "But for that scholarship, I wouldn't be here today."

✏️ Controlled Practice 15 min
Activity 1 · Complete the mixed conditional
  1. If I ___ (take) that job, I ___ (live) in Berlin now.
  2. If she ___ (not / be) so disorganized, she ___ (not / miss) the deadline last week.
  3. If they ___ (save) more in their twenties, they ___ (be) able to buy a house today.
  4. If I ___ (speak) better German, I ___ (apply) for that role last month.
Answer key · Activity 1
  1. had taken … 'd be living / would live (Mixed A: past → present)
  2. weren't … wouldn't have missed (Mixed B: present → past)
  3. had saved … would be (Mixed A)
  4. spoke … would have applied (Mixed B)
Activity 2 · 3rd conditional or mixed? — choose the natural ending
  1. If I had gone to bed earlier, …  a) I wouldn't have been late.  b) I wouldn't be so tired now. (the result is now) → ___
  2. If we had booked earlier, …  a) we would have paid half the price.  b) we would be at the hotel now. (the result is past) → ___
  3. If he weren't afraid of flying, …  a) he would have come with us last year.  b) he would have been a pilot. (present cause → past result) → ___
Answer key · Activity 2

1-b (mixed: past cause → present result) · 2-a (3rd: past → past) · 3-a (mixed: present cause → past result)

⭐ Model · talking about a turning point

Iris: Do you ever regret turning down that job in Madrid?

Tom: Sometimes. If I'd taken it, I'd probably be fluent in Spanish by now — and I'd be living somewhere sunnier. I could kick myself occasionally.

Iris: But you met Sara here.

Tom: Exactly, and that's the thing — if I weren't such a home bird, I'd have moved years ago and never met her. So, with hindsight, it was a turning point. It's water under the bridge now, honestly. I look back on it with no regrets.

Teacher note: both mixed types appear — If I'd taken it, I'd be fluent now (past → present) and If I weren't such a home bird, I'd have moved (present → past). Have the student find and label each one before they speak.
🗣️ Free Practice · Produce 15 min

Your turning point. Talk for two minutes about a decision that changed your life (a move, a job, a course, a relationship). Say how things would be different now if you'd chosen otherwise — using at least three mixed conditionals — then say whether you have any regrets.

One of the biggest turning points was when …. If I had(n't) …, I would (not) be … now. And if I weren't …, I would(n't) have …. With hindsight, ….
Model answer · a turning point

"The biggest turning point was probably when I turned down a place at university in the capital and stayed in my hometown instead. If I'd taken it, I'd almost certainly be working in finance now — and I'd probably be earning a good deal more. There are days when I could kick myself. But if I weren't the kind of person who needs family nearby, I'd have moved years ago and I'd never have met my partner. And if I hadn't stayed, I wouldn't be running my own business today either. So with hindsight, it's hard to call it a mistake. I look back on it now with almost no regrets — it's water under the bridge."

Three mixed conditionals, and note the two directions: If I'd taken it… I'd be working (past cause → present result) and If I weren't… I'd have moved (present cause → past result). The day's word, idiom and phrasal verb all appear naturally.

Teacher note: the target error to hunt is "If I would have…". Also push them to keep the two time frames straight — ask "and is that true now, or back then?"
📌 Homework
Write two paragraphs (150–180 words) about a turning point in your life: what happened, and how your life would be different today if you'd decided otherwise. Use at least three mixed conditionals and today's word, idiom and phrasal verb.
